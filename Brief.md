## 1. The commercial problem

Cyan is a 25-year, ISO-accredited, blue-chip-trusted merchandise and print management business. The website sells like a promotional products shop.

That gap is the whole project.

Right now the site speaks to someone buying 200 branded bottles for a trade stand. Fast turnaround, ships globally, wide product range, get a quote. That buyer is real, and worth having — but they're a £400 order, they're price-shopping, and they cost an account manager an afternoon.

The buyer worth £5,000–£50,000 a year is a different person entirely. She's a marketing manager, brand lead or procurement specialist with a recurring operational headache: five suppliers, no stock visibility, brand execution drifting, an ESG box she has to evidence, and a finance team asking why print spend looks like that. She isn't shopping for products. She's looking for someone to take the category off her desk and run it properly.

**The site is not underperforming because the SEO is weak. The SEO is weak because the site is aimed one tier below the money.** More optimisation on the current positioning just delivers more of the wrong enquiries, faster.

We fix the position, then we fix the rankings. In that order.

## 3. Who we're building for

### Primary buyer — "The Programme Owner"

- Marketing Manager, Brand Manager, Internal Comms Lead, or Procurement/Category Manager
- Company: 200–5,000 staff, or a multi-site/franchise operation
- Sectors Cyan already has proof in: pharmaceutical, healthcare, retail, entertainment, leisure — plus financial services and higher education as expansion targets
- Annual category spend: £5k–£50k+
- Buys on: reliability, brand control, supplier consolidation, sustainability evidence, and not getting fired
- Buys after: a call, a proposal, and usually a sample or a pilot project

**What she is afraid of:** a supplier who's brilliant on the first order and shaky on the fifth. Stock arriving off-brand. Having no answer when Sustainability asks for the audit trail. Being the person who chose the cheap option.

### Secondary buyer — "The One-Off"

Small event, small budget, one order. **We do not reject them — we route them.** They get a self-serve quote path, no calendar link, no AM assigned until the order justifies it.

### Anti-persona

Anyone whose first and only question is unit price on a single SKU. The site should let them self-select out inside 15 seconds.

## 4. Positioning and messaging

### The split-path model

Two front doors, one business. Confirmed direction.

```
                        CYAN
                          |
        ┌─────────────────┴─────────────────┐
        │                                   │
  BRANDED MERCHANDISE                 PRINT MANAGEMENT
  Programmes, stores,                 Procurement, production,
  onboarding kits, events             literature, POS, logistics
        │                                   │
        └─────────────────┬─────────────────┘
                          │
              ONE ACCOUNT. ONE CONTACT.
              ONE AUDIT TRAIL.
```

The split isn't just navigation — it's the SEO architecture, the case study taxonomy, and the sales qualification, all in one move. A visitor tells us which business they are by which door they walk through, and we can serve, track and convert them differently from that click onward.

The join at the bottom is the upsell engine. Merch clients discover print. Print clients discover merch. That's how a £5k account becomes a £25k account.

### Positioning statement (internal, not for the page)

> For marketing and procurement teams who are managing branded merchandise and print across too many suppliers, Cyan runs the whole category as a managed programme — sourcing, branding, storage, fulfilment and reporting — through one accredited team with 25 years of blue-chip delivery behind it.

### Messaging hierarchy

| Level | Message | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Promise | Your merchandise and print, run properly, by one team | Homepage hero |
| Mechanism | Consolidate suppliers → control brand → evidence sustainability → report spend | Homepage section 2, both pillar pages |
| Proof | 25 years, ISO 9001, ISO 14001, named blue-chip clients, Trustpilot | Persistent proof strip + dedicated credentials page |
| Risk reversal | Discovery call, no obligation, sample pack, pilot project option | Every CTA cluster |
| Action | Book a discovery call | Sticky, everywhere |

### Copy principles

- Write like a person who's done this for 25 years, not like a brochure
- Specifics beat adjectives. "Delivered 14,000 onboarding kits to 22 countries" beats "exceptional global delivery"
- Never say "solutions" where a real noun exists
- Every claim carries evidence within one scroll of itself
- Active voice, sentence case, no filler
- Buttons say what happens: "Book a discovery call", not "Get started"

### The face of the brand

Sian is on the front of the full catalogue. That's an asset the site is currently wasting.

A £5k+ buyer is making a relationship decision, not a product decision. Faceless supplier sites all read the same — same stock photography, same "our team" page, same "we pride ourselves on". A named, visible person cuts through that instantly, because it signals accountability: *there is someone here whose name is on this*.

**The principle: where relevant, not everywhere.** Sian appears at the moments where a human presence changes the outcome, and nowhere else. Overuse turns a credibility asset into a vanity one.

| Where | How | Why it works there |
|---|---|---|
| Catalogue / capability pack | Cover, consistent with print | Already the case — the site should match, not contradict, the printed collateral |
| Book-a-call page | Photo + one line: who you'll actually speak to | Removes the "who am I emailing?" hesitation at the exact point of conversion |
| About page | Lead voice, first person | This is where the personal story belongs |
| Insights / articles | Bylined, with a portrait | Author authority — this is what makes content rank *and* convert |
| Video / talking-head content | Direct to camera, on pillar pages | Highest-trust format there is, and the one competitors won't do |
| One homepage moment | A short signed statement, not a hero takeover | Presence without making the company look like a one-woman band |

**Where Sian does not appear:** service pages, sector pages, case studies, the credentials page. Those pages must read as *the company* — procurement is buying an organisation with ISO certification and 25 years behind it, not a personality. Mixing the two dilutes both.

The balance to hold: personal brand opens the door, corporate credentials get the contract signed. The site needs both, doing different jobs, in different places.

### 5.1 The online catalogue — its actual job

`cyan-group.promotionalcatalogue.co.uk` is a white-labelled platform (Page Partnership theme, built by Supersonic Playground) carrying roughly a dozen product categories. It is **set to `noindex, nofollow`** — which is correct, and should stay that way. Syndicated catalogue content duplicated across every distributor on the same platform is a duplicate-content liability, not an asset. It will never rank, and it shouldn't try.

That means two things.

**One: every bit of SEO value has to be earned on cyan-group.com.** The catalogue contributes nothing and never will. Don't budget for it, don't wait on it.

**Two: the catalogue is your self-serve tier, and it's genuinely useful in that role.** It's exactly the right destination for the "One-Off" buyer in §3 — the small event order that shouldn't consume an account manager's afternoon. Formalise it:

| Decision | Action |
|---|---|
| Homepage primary CTA | "Book a discovery call". **Not** "To products" |
| Homepage secondary CTA | "Browse the catalogue" — clearly secondary, visually quieter |
| Sub-£5k enquiries at `/book-a-call/` | Routed here automatically, with the catalogue framed as the fast self-serve path — not as a rejection |
| Domain | Map to `catalogue.cyan-group.com` via CNAME if the platform supports it. Keeps the buyer inside your brand and stops the URL looking like a third party's |
| Return path | Prominent link back to cyan-group.com from the catalogue header, so browsers who turn out to be programme buyers can find their way to a call |
| Contact details | Must match the main site exactly — see audit item 20 |

**Facts currently locked behind that `noindex` that belong on the main site:**

- **Express products — delivered in 5 days or less.** Answers a real objection, and "fast turnaround branded merchandise" is a searched term
- **Made in the UK — over 300 products.** Directly relevant to procurement teams with UK-sourcing or social-value requirements
- **BPMA Product of the Year** range — a third-party award you can point to
- **600+ new products this year** — evidence the range is actively curated

Every one of these is a genuine selling point sitting on a page Google has been told to ignore. They belong on indexable service pages.

### 6.5 Case studies (`/work/[slug]/`)

Six needed at launch. Non-negotiable structure:

**Client / Challenge / What Cyan did / Result (with numbers) / Quote / Services used / Sector**

### 6.6 Credentials page

This is the page that gets forwarded to procurement. It should be genuinely useful and slightly boring:

- **BPMA Chartered Distributor** — explain what it means in one plain sentence, because most buyers won't know
- Insurance levels, GDPR position, supplier audit process, ethical sourcing policy, social value statement, standard payment terms, references on request

Plus a single **"Download the tender pack"** button gated behind a short form. This page should be genuinely useful and slightly boring — it's the page that gets forwarded to procurement, and it's the answer to "send me your accreditations", which right now has nowhere to point.

### 6.7 Book a call

Not a contact form. A qualifier.

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Name, company, email, phone | Basic |
| What do you need? (Merchandise / Print / Both) | Routing |

## 7. SEO strategy

### 7.1 The strategic shift

We stop fighting for product-intent terms. "Corporate gifts" and "promotional products" SERPs are dominated by catalogue e-commerce with page depth Cyan can't out-publish, and the traffic converts at low value anyway.

We go after **service-intent** terms: lower volume, far higher value, and the incumbents there (Hague, Commercial, Trident et al.) win on process language and procurement credibility — which Cyan actually has and simply isn't publishing.

### 7.2 Keyword → page map

| Priority | Target keyword cluster | Landing page | Intent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | print management company / print management services UK | `/print-management/` | Service |
| 2 | branded merchandise agency / promotional merchandise agency | `/branded-merchandise/` | Service |
| 3 | print procurement services / print buying services | `/print-management/print-procurement/` | Service |
| 4 | company merchandise store / employee merchandise store | `/branded-merchandise/company-merchandise-store/` | Service |
| 5 | employee onboarding kits / new starter packs UK | `/branded-merchandise/onboarding-kits/` | Service |
| 6 | sustainable promotional merchandise / eco branded merchandise | `/branded-merchandise/sustainable-merchandise/` | Service |
| 7 | merchandise fulfilment / storage and distribution | `/print-management/storage-and-fulfilment/` | Service |
| 8 | branded clothing supplier / corporate workwear branding | `/branded-merchandise/branded-clothing/` | Service |
| 9 | exhibition and event merchandise | `/branded-merchandise/event-merchandise/` | Service |
| 10 | pharmaceutical print management / pharma merchandise | `/sectors/pharmaceutical/` | Sector |
| 11 | retail POS print management | `/print-management/large-format-and-pos/` | Service |
| 12 | branded merchandise Birmingham / print management Birmingham | `/` + GBP | Local |

### 7.3 Content engine

Twelve articles in the first six months, each mapped to a pillar and each answering a question the Programme Owner actually types:

- What does print management actually cost, and where do the savings come from?
- How to consolidate five merchandise suppliers into one without losing control
- The merchandise questions your sustainability team will ask (and how to answer them)
- Building an employee merchandise store: what it takes and what it costs
- Onboarding kits: what to include, what to skip, what people actually keep
- Print procurement for regulated industries — the artwork approval problem
- What ISO 14001 means when you're buying promotional products
- Lead times: a realistic calendar for Q4 merchandise
- How to write a merchandise RFP that gets you comparable quotes
- Brand consistency across 40 sites: a print management case
- Social value in procurement — what counts and how to evidence it
- The real cost of cheap merchandise (returns, waste, brand damage)

Every article ends with a contextual CTA to the relevant pillar page, not a generic contact link.

### 7.4 Technical SEO — non-negotiables

- **Crawl access check first.** Both `cyan-group.com` and `www.cyan-group.com` returned 401 to an external fetch during research. This may be a firewall or bot rule. Verify in Search Console before anything else — if legitimate crawlers are being bounced, that's a free win.
- **Full redirect map.** Legacy URL patterns exist (e.g. `/sub-solution/…`). Every old URL gets a 301 to its closest new equivalent. No 404s, no redirect chains. This is the single biggest risk in the project — losing 25 years of accumulated authority to sloppy redirects.
- One H1 per page. Descriptive, keyword-bearing, human.
- Title tags ≤60 chars, meta descriptions ≤155, written by a human for a click, not stuffed.
- Schema: `Organization`, `LocalBusiness`, `Service` on every service page, `FAQPage` on every page with an FAQ, `BreadcrumbList` sitewide, `Article` on insights.
- XML sitemap, auto-generated, split by content type. Clean `robots.txt`.
- Canonical tags on every page. Self-referencing by default.
- Image alt text that describes the image, written for a person using a screen reader.
- Internal linking follows §5's rule. Build it into the CMS, not into someone's memory.
- **NAP consistency.** One trading address, one phone number, applied identically across cyan-group.com, the catalogue, Google Business Profile, Trustpilot, LinkedIn and every directory listing. Currently there are three locations and a London number in circulation (audit item 20). Inconsistent NAP directly suppresses local rankings.
- **Google Business Profile** claimed, categorised as "Promotional Products Supplier" and "Printing Service", with photos, services and posts. This is most of the "near me" ranking battle.
- **Leave the catalogue on `noindex, nofollow`.** It's the right call for syndicated product content. Link to it normally from the main site; don't try to make it rank.
- Core Web Vitals budget in §10.3.

## 8. Design direction

### 8.1 The idea — the catalogue is already the answer

**The 2026|2027 catalogue cover is the best expression of this brand that exists.** Black-and-white photography with **selective cyan** — the hair, the cup lid — against clean white, with the CYAN wordmark stacked 2×2 in alternating solid and outline letters. It's confident, editorial, and unmistakably one company.

The website is a different animal: black-led, heavy, cyan barely present. Neither is wrong. But they're not the same brand, and the catalogue is the better one.

So the design direction is simple: **bring the site to the catalogue, not the other way round.**

Three moves:

1. **Selective-cyan photography becomes the signature treatment.** B&W images with one cyan element brought back to full colour. You already own it, it's genuinely distinctive, and it puts cyan into the largest element on every page without touching the black foundation. This is the answer to "cyan shouldn't be a minor accent" — and it came from your own material.
2. **Keep black, but give it a job.** Black for heroes, nav and section breaks — drama and premium feel. White/stock for content sections — readability. The selective-cyan photography is the through-line that makes both feel like one system rather than two.
3. **Lock the palette.** Purple, red and yellow powder bursts, pink links — all of it goes. The catalogue's restraint is exactly why it looks expensive.

### 8.2 What we keep and what we change

| Existing device | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Black nav bar and black heroes | **Keep** | Premium, confident, distinctive in a sector full of white-and-blue supplier sites |
| Solid + outline word pairing in headlines (`BRANDED` / *MERCH &*) | **Keep and systematise** | Genuinely ownable. Make it a documented rule: the *subject* is solid, the *category* is outlined |
| CMYK gradient strip as section divider | **Cut** | A four-colour rainbow bar is print-trade shorthand, not brand. Every print shop in Britain has used one. It signals "we operate a press" to a buyer who doesn't care about the press — and it's the one element that would look wrong sitting next to the catalogue. See §8.4 for what replaces it |
| Printing-press hero on `/print/` | **Keep — and steal it for the homepage** | Far stronger than the homepage's generic person-holding-a-bottle shot. It's the most "we actually do this" image on the site |
| B&W team portraits | **Keep** | Cohesive, and they age well |
| ISO / EcoVadis badge cluster | **Keep, move up** | These are procurement currency — see §6.6 |
| Trustpilot bar at the very top | **Keep** | Proof before the fold, correct instinct |
| Sparkle glyph in the wordmark | **Keep** | Small, distinctive, fine |
| Rounded-corner image cards | **Keep** | Consistent, modern |
| Purple particle-burst graphic (team page) | **Cut** | Off-palette and off-strategy. If the effect is wanted, it's a cyan ink dispersion on black — one colour, not a firework |
| "TO PRODUCTS" as dominant hero CTA | **Change** | This is the positioning problem in one button — see §6.1 |
| "AI VIDEO" in primary nav | **Demote** | Dilutes the proposition for a £5k+ merchandise/print buyer. Move to a sub-page or the footer |
| US flag on the chat widget | **Change to UK** | Small thing, real trust wobble on a Birmingham business selling to UK procurement |

### 8.3 Tokens

**Colour — black-led, cyan-structural**

| Token | Hex | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `--black` | `#000000` | Nav, hero fields, dark sections. The base. Roughly 50% of the site's surface area |
| `--cyan` | `#00AEEF` | Process cyan. **Every primary CTA, every rule, every active state, all icon fills, outline-type strokes, section labels** |
| `--cyan-deep` | `#0076A8` | Cyan for small text and links on light backgrounds — passes AA where `--cyan` doesn't |
| `--cyan-wash` | `#E2F4FC` | Tinted panels, cards, form fields, table stripes on light sections |
| `--ink` | `#0E1113` | Body text on light backgrounds |
| `--stock` | `#F4F6F7` | Light section background |
| `--paper` | `#FFFFFF` | Cards, elevated surfaces |
| `--alert` | `#EC008C` | Functional only — form errors and destructive confirmations. Never decorative |

**The discipline — this is what "cyan is not a minor accent" means in practice:**

- **Selective-cyan photography is the signature.** Every contextual image is black-and-white with exactly one element restored to cyan. One element, never two. This is the catalogue treatment applied sitewide, and it's the highest-leverage colour decision available. Product and merchandise photography stays full colour — clients need to see the actual goods.
- **Outline type gets a cyan stroke, not a white one.** `BRANDED` stays white solid; *MERCH &* becomes a cyan outline. Costs nothing, puts cyan into the biggest element on the page.
- **Every primary CTA is a cyan fill.** Currently they're outlined with a thin cyan edge. Fill them. One learnable action colour, sitewide.
- **All rules, dividers, list markers and section labels are cyan.**
- **Dark sections get a cyan edge-light** — a soft glow along one edge or behind the type, so black sections don't read flat.
- **Section boundaries are colour changes, not devices.** Black to stock to black. The transition *is* the boundary — it costs nothing, it lets the black do the dramatic work, and it needs no ornament. Minor boundaries get a 1px cyan rule with a mono label in the margin. That's the whole system.

**Colour lockdown.** Black, white, cyan, two neutrals, one functional alert colour. That's the entire palette — and it's the catalogue's palette, which is the point. No purple, no red powder bursts, no pink links, no rainbow. The catalogue looks like a £10k brand precisely because it says one colour, loudly, and nothing else. Very few companies own a colour. Don't share it.

**Type — Poppins, confirmed**

Poppins throughout. Free on Google Fonts, available as a variable font, and it already carries the brand equity. No change, no licence, no risk.

| Role | Face | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Display | **Poppins** 700/800, uppercase | Negative tracking, line-height 1.0. Solid/outline pairing per §8.2. Outline via `-webkit-text-stroke` with a `paint-order` fallback |
| Body | **Poppins** 400/500 | Left-aligned always over two lines. Line-height 1.6, max measure 68 characters |
| Utility | **Geist Mono** — *the one addition* | Captions, stats, section labels, spec detail. Job-ticket typography — it's what makes the site read as a production business rather than a shop |

Self-host both via `next/font`, variable, subset to Latin, preloaded. Poppins at four weights maximum (400, 500, 700, 800) — more than that is wasted bytes.

Type scale: `12 / 14 / 16 / 18 / 21 / 28 / 38 / 52 / 72 / 96`. Display line-height 1.0 with negative tracking; body 1.6.

**Layout**

12-column grid, 1440px max content width, 80px gutters desktop / 20px mobile. Vertical rhythm 120px desktop / 64px mobile between major sections.

**Section boundaries.** Major transitions are colour-block changes — black to stock, stock to black. Minor transitions are a 1px `--cyan` rule with a mono label sitting in the left margin. Two devices, both structural, neither decorative.

### 8.4 Signature element

The hero headline's outline word **draws itself into existence.** The cyan stroke traces left to right via `stroke-dashoffset` over roughly 700ms, then settles. The solid word is already there; the outline arrives.

It's built entirely from the brand's own type device and entirely in cyan. No fourth colour, no press furniture, nothing that needs explaining. It reads as precision rather than as a printing demonstration — which is the difference between looking like a partner and looking like a supplier.

First page load only, once per session, skipped entirely under `prefers-reduced-motion`. It is the only decorative animation on the site.

## 9. Motion specification

### 9.1 The rule

Every animation has a job title. If it can't be named, it's cut.

**Global easing tokens:**

```css
--ease-out:     cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1);      /* entering, exiting */
--ease-in-out:  cubic-bezier(0.77, 0, 0.175, 1);      /* on-screen movement */
--ease-drawer:  cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1);       /* mobile nav */
```

Never `ease-in` on UI. Never `transition: all`. Nothing enters from `scale(0)` — real objects don't appear from nothing; enter from `scale(0.96)` + `opacity: 0`.

### 9.2 The spec

| Element | Trigger | Animation | Duration | Easing | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hero outline draw | First load only | Cyan stroke traces left→right via `stroke-dashoffset` | 700ms | `--ease-out` | The one signature moment |
| Outline headings | Scroll into view | Same stroke draw, shorter | 500ms | `--ease-out` | Extends the brand's own type device without adding a new one |
| Section rule | Scroll into view | 1px cyan rule draws left→right | 400ms | `--ease-out` | Marks a real section boundary |
| Nav | Scroll past 80px | Background solidifies, height reduces 88→64px | 200ms | `--ease-out` | Preventing jarring change |
| Split cards | Hover (desktop only) | Lift 4px, sub-services fade + slide in 8px | 220ms | `--ease-out` | State indication |
| Process steps | Scroll into view | Sequential reveal, 60ms stagger, 12px rise | 300ms | `--ease-out` | Explanation — shows sequence |
| Stat counters | Scroll into view, once | Digits roll to value | 800ms | `--ease-out` | Makes scale land |
| Case study cards | Scroll into view | Fade + 16px rise, 50ms stagger | 300ms | `--ease-out` | Prevents pop-in |
| Client logo strip | Continuous | Marquee, pauses on hover | — | `linear` | Ambient proof |
| Buttons | `:active` | `scale(0.97)` | 120ms | `--ease-out` | Feedback |
| Buttons | `:hover` | Background shift only | 160ms | `ease` | Feedback |
| Form fields | Focus | Border colour + 2px cyan focus ring | 150ms | `--ease-out` | Feedback + a11y |
| Accordion / FAQ | Click | Height + opacity | 200ms | `--ease-out` | Prevents jarring change |
| Mobile nav | Tap | Slide from right, 100%→0 | 300ms in / 200ms out | `--ease-drawer` | Spatial consistency |
| Page transition | Route change | Cross-fade only | 180ms | `--ease-out` | Prevents flash |
| Toast (form success) | Submit | Sonner default | — | — | Feedback |

### 9.3 What we are explicitly NOT doing

- No parallax backgrounds
- No autoplaying hero video (it costs LCP and says nothing)
- No scroll-jacking, no horizontal scroll hijack
- No text scrambling / typewriter effects
- No animated gradient meshes
- No 3D anything
- No entrance animation on any element the user will see more than a few times per session
- No animation longer than 300ms on any interactive UI element

### 9.4 Accessibility of motion

`prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` keeps opacity and colour transitions (they aid comprehension), removes all transform-based movement, and skips the hero outline draw entirely — the outline is simply present on load.

Hover animations gated behind `@media (hover: hover) and (pointer: fine)` so touch devices don't fire false hover states on tap.

## 10. Technical build specification

### 10.1 Stack

| Layer | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Framework | **Next.js 15, App Router, TypeScript** | SSG/ISR for SEO, server components, first-class Vercel deploy |
| Styling | **Tailwind CSS** + `cva` + `clsx` | cva for typed component variants, clsx for conditional classes |
| UI primitives | **base-ui** | Accessible dialogs, popovers, accordions with focus trapping handled |
| Motion | **motion** (Framer Motion) for scroll-linked and interruptible; **plain CSS transitions** for hover, focus, and simple state | CSS runs off the main thread and survives page-load jank; JS only where it earns it |
| Numbers | **NumberFlow** | Stat counters, done properly |
| Toasts | **Sonner** | Form feedback |
| CMS | **Sanity** (headless) | Marketing team can edit without a developer; strong structured content model for the case-study/sector taxonomy |
| Forms | Next.js server actions → **HubSpot** (or Resend if no CRM) | Routing logic lives server-side |
| Booking | **HubSpot Meetings** or **Cal.com**, lazy-loaded below the fold | Never let a third-party embed hurt LCP |
| Analytics | GA4 + Search Console + a privacy-first alternative if preferred | Conversion tracking on the qualified-call event specifically |
| Hosting | Vercel | ISR, edge caching, image optimisation |

### 10.1a Build route — Lovable or Claude Code

Both are viable now. The SEO objection to Lovable that would have killed it a year ago has largely gone.

<cite index="27-3">Lovable apps created from May 2026 onwards run on TanStack Start with server-side rendering, returning fully rendered HTML on every request, while older React and Vite projects get on-request pre-rendering for verified crawlers.</cite> <cite index="22-1">The May 2026 release also bundled automatic pre-rendering, Semrush data in the chat, and an on-demand SEO review across all plans.</cite> <cite index="21-1">Sitemaps and schema now ship out of the box too.</cite>

So the decision isn't about whether Google can read it any more. It's about four things:

| Factor | Lovable | Claude Code |
|---|---|---|
| Speed to something you can look at | Hours | Days |
| Visual iteration with a non-developer in the loop | Excellent | Slower loop |
| **301 redirect map from legacy URLs** | Limited platform control — may need Cloudflare rules in front | Full control in `next.config.js` |
| CMS for a marketing team to edit | Supabase-backed, workable but you're building the editing experience | Sanity, purpose-built, no dev needed after launch |
| Exact motion spec (§9) | Achievable, but harder to hold precisely | Direct — the spec is written to be implemented literally |
| Framework | TanStack Start (no Next.js option) | Next.js 15 |
| Long-term ownership | Platform-coupled | Plain repo, host anywhere |

**Recommended route — use both, for what each is best at:**

1. **Lovable for direction-finding.** Build two or three homepage directions fast, look at them properly, decide the cyan balance and the hero treatment with your eyes rather than in a document. This is the fastest way to resolve the design questions that are hard to settle in writing.
2. **Claude Code for production.** Once the direction is locked, build it properly in Next.js — because of the redirect map, mainly. Twenty-five years of domain authority is the single most valuable asset in this project, and it moves or dies on 301s you can control precisely.

If speed genuinely matters more than the redirect precision, going Lovable-only is defensible — but put Cloudflare in front for redirect rules, and budget time for the SEO configuration Lovable still won't do for you: keyword strategy, internal linking discipline, and the content itself.

### 10.2 CMS content models

`Service`, `Pillar`, `Sector`, `CaseStudy`, `Insight`, `TeamMember`, `Credential`, `Testimonial`, `FAQ`, `SiteSettings`.

Case studies reference services and sectors so the `/work/` filter and all "related" modules build themselves. Internal linking becomes structural, not manual.

### 10.3 Performance budget

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| LCP | < 2.0s |
| INP | < 200ms |
| CLS | < 0.05 |
| JS on landing routes | < 180KB gzipped |
| Lighthouse Performance | ≥ 95 mobile |
| Images | AVIF/WebP via `next/image`, explicit dimensions always |
| Fonts | Self-hosted variable, `next/font`, `font-display: swap`, preloaded |

### 10.4 Accessibility floor

WCAG 2.1 AA. Visible keyboard focus on every interactive element (2px cyan ring, 2px offset). Logical focus order. Skip-to-content link. All colour pairs ≥4.5:1 for body, ≥3:1 for large text.

**Critical contrast rule:** `--cyan` (#00AEEF) is a *field* colour, not a *text* colour on light backgrounds. Use it for full-bleed sections, buttons, fills, rules and large display type. For any small text, link or icon sitting on `--stock` or `--paper`, use `--cyan-deep`. White text on a `--cyan` field is fine at 18px+ / bold; below that, use `--cyan-dark` as the field instead. Build this into the `Button` and `Link` cva variants so it can't be got wrong later. Forms have real `<label>`s. Errors announced via `aria-live`.

## 12. The £10k standard

£10,000 is the **quality bar**, not the invoice. It's the answer to "how good does this need to be?" — and the answer is: indistinguishable from a site a good independent studio would charge ten grand to produce.

That standard is what the following list defends. If a shortcut breaks one of these, it isn't a shortcut, it's a downgrade.

| A £10k site has | A £2k site has |
|---|---|
| A positioning strategy the design is derived from | A nice template with the logo dropped in |
| A design system — tokens, variants, states — used consistently | Ad-hoc styling, drift between pages |
| Bespoke type pairing and a real colour discipline | Default system fonts and a primary/secondary swatch |
| One signature moment nobody else has | Stock animation library, applied everywhere |
| Motion specced with durations, easings and a stated purpose | `transition: all 300ms` |
| Content architecture built for the SEO map | Pages made up as they were needed |
| A tested redirect map preserving 25 years of authority | Whatever the platform did automatically |
| Case studies with numbers | Testimonials with adjectives |
| Real photography of real work | Stock images of people in headsets |
| Lighthouse ≥95, WCAG 2.1 AA, verified | "It looks fine on my laptop" |
| A qualification mechanism that protects the team's time | A contact form |

**Where the money-equivalent effort actually goes**, in rough proportion — useful for deciding what to protect if time gets tight:

| Workstream | Share of effort |
|---|---|
| Discovery, positioning, messaging | 15% |
| SEO strategy, technical audit, redirect map | 12% |
| Design system and key templates | 25% |
| Build and CMS modelling | 30% |
| Motion implementation and QA | 8% |
| Content production support | 6% |
| Testing, launch, analytics | 4% |

**The two things worth paying real money for regardless of how the site gets built:** photography (of actual work, actual team, actual merchandise) and case study material. Everything else on this list can be produced with AI tooling and taste. Those two can't be faked, and they're what separate a £10k-looking site from a £10k-*performing* one.
